# Where Shared Sky goes next

Shared Sky was built in one weekend for YOHAKU 余白. This page lists what it could become, and the principles that should guide it. None of it is built yet.

## 1. Skies shared by the communities who hold them

The sky cultures in Shared Sky today come from open Stellarium datasets. Many skies are missing: Mapuche, Lakota, Maya Kaqchikel, Shuar, Gadigal, Samburu and many more. They should be added only if those communities choose to, and on their terms.

The next version should make that possible without anyone else in between:

- **Communities upload their own sky:** figures, names in their own language, stories, artwork and sound recordings, through a simple form. No coding needed.
- **They decide who sees what:** public, shared only with invited people, or kept private. Some knowledge is not for everyone, and the tool should respect that by design.
- **Their data can live on their own server.** Shared Sky would read a sky from a link the community controls, so they can change it or take it down at any time. Nothing is copied or kept without consent.
- **Following the CARE principles** for Indigenous data governance (Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics), built with communities rather than for them.

## 2. Listening to the sky

A prototype idea: turn the sky into sound. Each star becomes a note on a pentatonic scale. Its brightness sets how loud it is, its colour sets the timbre, and its position sets the pitch and where you hear it from. Satellites and debris crossing a constellation would add their own sounds, so you could hear how busy a sky has become, not only see it. Communities could also attach their own songs to their figures.

## 3. Seven generations, in more depth

The "Seven generations in orbit" section is a first, rough step. Next:

- **Better lifetimes**, using each object's measured drag and the solar cycle instead of a rule of thumb by height.
- **Uncertainty shown honestly:** where an object might be, not just a single dot.
- **Where things come down:** which lands and seas re-entering objects are likely to fall on, and whose Country that is.
- **Close approaches:** which objects come near each other, and who carries the risk.

## 4. Art tied to places

Let artists and communities attach artworks, stories or recordings to a place and a moment, so the map becomes a shared atlas of how people see their sky.

## 5. Beyond the hackathon

Shared Sky is open source (MIT for the code). Anyone can run it, adapt it, or host a version for their own community. Ideas, corrections and requests are welcome through GitHub issues, and requests from knowledge holders come first.
